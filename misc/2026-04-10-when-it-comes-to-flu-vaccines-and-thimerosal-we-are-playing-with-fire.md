# When It Comes to Flu Vaccines and Thimerosal, We Are Playing With Fire

IN THE EARLY days of the COVID-19 pandemic, I stood in makeshift COVID-19 field hospitals (known as ‘Alternative Care Sites’) in New York City, bearing witness to a public health crisis that tested every fiber of our nation’s resilience. At the time, we knew very little. We lacked access to proper testing and treatment. We lacked contact tracing systems and reliable personal protective equipment. We did have large hotels, which ultimately became areas where we isolated and quarantined residents. But perhaps what we lacked most, and what continues to elude us, was trust.

Trust in the science. Trust in the systems. Trust in each other.

Today, we find ourselves at another inflection point. The conversation around the flu vaccine has been reignited with controversy due to renewed attention on thimerosal, a mercury-based preservative used in multidose vials. Despite decades of evidence showing no causal link between thimerosal and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, its invocation once again signals how deeply distrust has rooted itself in our public discourse.

And this time, it’s not just fringe actors fanning the flames. It’s policymakers. It’s media personalities. It’s individuals in positions of immense influence who have replaced evidence with ideology and expertise with conspiracy.

[to Flu Vaccines](https://antimicrobial-hub.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/9155452694.html)

Make no mistake – this is not a drill and we are playing with fire.

When We Politicize Science, People Suffer

As someone who was nominated to the CDC’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), I was alarmed to see the recent ousting of seasoned experts in favor of individuals lacking basic public health training, some even openly antagonistic to vaccines. This isn’t just a staffing change. It’s a structural attack on a foundational principle of public health: that science should inform policy, not the other way around.

Vaccination is more than a shot in the arm. It is a social contract. Undermining it has cascading effects, particularly for those already burdened by structural inequities: communities of color, undocumented families, low-income populations. When these groups experience disinformation on top of poor access, the outcome is predictable: lower vaccination rates, rising outbreaks, and a catastrophic breakdown in trust. And once trust is lost, it can take generations to rebuild.

Let me be clear: the current attacks on science are not limited to COVID-19 or the flu. Today it is mRNA vaccines. Tomorrow it may be HPV, MMR, or routine pediatric immunizations. This isn’t about one vaccine or one virus. It’s about whether we choose to base our decisions on evidence and ethics, or on fear and political gain.

The implications extend beyond patient behavior. If scientific consensus can be undermined so easily, what message does that send to the next generation of researchers? To the pharmaceutical companies navigating vaccine development pipelines? Innovation cannot thrive in a climate where scientific inquiry is treated as suspect.

In my decades of work from refugee camps to homeless encampments, from South Sudan to Skid Row, I have seen what happens when health systems crumble. I’ve held malnourished children whose mothers walked for miles to reach care. I’ve watched epidemics unravel entire villages because of a lack of trust in vaccines brought in by outsiders. In every case, rebuilding trust took time, humility, and community partnership.

It’s no different here.

During the rollout of the COVID-19 vaccine in Marin County, California, my team noticed an early trend: those getting vaccinated were overwhelmingly white, well-off, and well-connected. In fact, we coined the phrase "the triple Cs": Caucasians with cars and computers. The people most at risk – Black, Latinx, undocumented, uninsured – were being left behind. The very people who had been hardest hit by COVID were the last to get protection.

And so we adapted. We brought vaccines directly to communities via mobile health units. We engaged trusted community health workers. We listened more than we spoke. And in the end, our most successful interventions weren’t just medical, they were relational.

This Is the Roadmap Forward

If we want to counter science distrust, we must stop viewing it as a communication problem and start seeing it as a structural one. Distrust is not irrational. It is often born of lived experience: of medical racism, of inaccessible care, of being treated as an afterthought. To earn trust, we must demonstrate trustworthiness. That means equitable access. That means transparent communication. That means lifting up the voices of those who have been systematically silenced.

This is not a time for neutrality. As public health professionals, scientists, and clinicians, we must speak plainly: disinformation kills. Conflating fringe conspiracies with scientific discourse is not balance. It is negligence. We cannot afford to wait until the next surge or the next scandal. The groundwork for health equity must be laid now, in the calm between storms.

Thimerosal is a case study in how misinformation can persist even in the face of overwhelming evidence. The CDC, FDA, and WHO have long affirmed its safety in the trace amounts used in vaccines. Yet, its specter still lingers in vaccine debates, not because of data but because of deeply held fears and strategic disinformation. That fear is not irrational, but it is weaponized. And we must counter it with both empathy and truth.

It is tempting to think that these culture wars around science will pass. But history tells us otherwise. The erosion of trust, once begun, becomes self-reinforcing. Each policy decision, each headline, each tweet either builds or erodes the social fabric that holds our public health system together.
