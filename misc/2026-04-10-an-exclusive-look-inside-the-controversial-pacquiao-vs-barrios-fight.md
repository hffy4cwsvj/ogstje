# An Exclusive Look Inside the Controversial Pacquiao vs. Barrios Fight

“Life is like boxing in many unsettling respects. But boxing is only like boxing.”—Joyce Carol Oates

NINE DAYS BEFORE THE FIGHT

THE TRAINER STANDS in the corner of the ring in his boxing gym. He scraped this business together from nothing, year by year over three decades, starting with a small room above a laundromat in a Hollywood strip mall. He built the first ring himself, measured it to regulation size, painted the floor, hung the ropes. When the space next door became vacant, he took that over, knocked down walls, built another ring. The gym grew. He took over the downstairs next, after the laundromat moved out. He took over the apartment on the other side of the parking lot and moved in.

He took over the parking lot itself.

He stands in the corner now, the king of this place, his place. His best fighter is here training for a big fight—a comeback, they’re calling it. This is the fighter he believed in, the man he built to win more titles in more divisions than any boxer ever.

The trainer, the great Freddie Roach, has been slowed by age and illness, and he can’t do everything for his fighter that he once did. Freddie was never a shouter, but now it’s difficult sometimes even to get a few words out. He used to catch mitts, but now, after decades and millions of punches thrown at Freddie’s hands, his fighter won’t let Freddie catch mitts anymore. The fighter is afraid he might hurt Freddie.

Freddie thinks this is bullshit. “I can catch anybody,” he says. But he respects his fighter and goes along with his decision.

The fighter is the great Manny Pacquiao. He hasn’t fought for four years, just as Lazarus had been dead for four days before Jesus performed the miracle of raising him up. Freddie is here to resurrect Manny’s career.

People say Manny is crazy to fight again and that Freddie is crazy to train him. They say it would be a miracle if Manny won this fight, a miracle even if he survived a few rounds before hitting the floor for the last time.

Freddie stands in the corner watching his fighter spar. Manny looks over for approval, for assurance, for the mere reminder that Freddie is there. Every few minutes, Manny—sweating, rippling, his body kinetic—hops over to his corner. He stands close to Freddie, their bodies almost touching. With knobby fingers Freddie unscrews the cap off a water bottle. Manny opens his mouth. Freddie gently holds the bottle to Manny’s lips and pours a dribble of water onto Manny’s tongue, holding his hand underneath to catch drips, Freddie’s own mouth contorting a little, involuntarily, as if he were drinking the water too. They don’t speak. It is what Freddie can do, and it is what Manny needs. Not the water—yes, the water, but more than that: the tender offer of the water, and the constancy of Freddie’s presence, raising him up, raising him up, raising him up.

THE NIGHT OF THE FIGHT

THE CHAMPION ENTERS the arena first, and everything about him is large. The towering headdresses of the costumed dancers swiveling and twirling their warrior dance before the champion emerges from the tunnel, faux feathers waving high above their heads. The entourage that surrounds him is large: an armada of heads and torsos floating down the path toward the ring, led by a man wearing a cowboy hat and rapping in Spanish and English. And the boxer himself, Mario Barrios, “El Azteca,” a nickname that conjures an empire. He’s six feet and mohawked, and his long arms dangle as he walks, arms that can reach a full four inches farther than those of his challenger.

There are a few muted cheers and a few distant boos for Barrios, but mostly the crowd emits the low white noise of antipathy. Barrios reaches the ring and languishes there, no one paying attention to him. Everyone’s got their phones up and aimed at the tunnel, from which the challenger will emerge next. The challenger is why they’re here.

And then the voice: “Ladies and gentlemen, please welcome boxing’s only eight-division world champion…”

Boom!

The cheer swells as instantly as fireworks, filling the space inside the MGM Grand in Las Vegas with chants of his name.

“Mann-ny! Mann-ny! Mann-ny!”

The Pride of the Philippines doesn’t walk in like Barrios did; he bounces down the path—bomp, bomp, bomp, bomp, his red gloves wiggling at the end of his short arms, loose jabs at the air. Manny Pacquiao is 5'5". His nickname is “Pac-Man,” a video game about a little yellow ball that eats dots that was popular before Barrios was born. Pacquiao is 46, which, to hear some of the unsmiling boxing writers in the press seats tell it, might as well be 106.

Forty-six is old in boxing. But also, guys drop dead of heart attacks at 46, playing golf or sleeping on the couch—boom, gone. And people say, So young.

Comebacks usually turn out to be a bad idea. Joe Louis was considered ancient in 1951 when, mired in tax debt at age 37, he staged a comeback and was pummeled out of the ring by Rocky Marciano. Muhammad Ali, then 38, fought a much younger Larry Holmes in 1980, and it was an embarrassment—the only time Ali ever lost on account of a fight being stopped for his safety, maybe even for his survival.

Looking out over the MGM Grand, one writer in the press seats, his sallow face alit by his laptop and his girth hanging over the folding chair, says, “It’s gonna be a short fight.”

Yesterday Jerry Izenberg, the 95-year-old sportswriter for the New Jersey Star-Ledger, wrote about Pacquiao that this is “a fight that nobody thinks he can win.” “I wish this fight was never made,” he wrote, worrying that Pacquiao could get destroyed. Izenberg, still working, lives in a ranch house out in hell-hot Henderson, Nevada, now, a few miles from the Vegas Strip. He’s watching on TV.

But this fight was made, and it’s happening, and there, shuffling fast a few steps behind Manny out of the tunnel, is Freddie. His Irish skin glows white in the spotlight, his white buzz cut too, and his white T-shirt with black block letters: wild card boxing club. Thick black glasses. The Parkinson’s draws his mouth down a little. He always looks as if he wants to say something but is holding back.

Some of them are wondering if THIS IS IT FOR FREDDIE. Maybe that’s why they’re cheering so loudly for him. PARKINSON’S IS TOUGH, and it’s MYSTERIOUS, and maybe their DAD or their AUNT had it and THEY’VE SEEN what it can do.

Manny couldn’t have done this, wouldn’t be here, without Freddie. For the last eight weeks he’s been preparing for this night at Wild Card, Freddie’s famous, shopworn gym in Hollywood, same as he has for every fight since 2001. Freddie came up as a fighter too, trained under the great Eddie Futch, then became a trainer himself. He’s worked with everyone. Mike Tyson, Marlon Starling, Miguel Cotto, Julio Cesar Chavez Jr., James Toney.

But Manny is his masterpiece.

Their bond, one of the tightest and most potent in all of sport, helped make Freddie as famous to boxing fans as the fighters themselves. “Hey, Freddie!” they call as he walks through the MGM’s gilt lobbies and across its carpeted casino floors. They want selfies, autographs. They stare at him from across the restaurant, or in the airport, trying to figure out if it’s really Freddie Roach, and sometimes he forgets how famous he is and he gets pissed. He thinks they might be making fun of him. What the fuck is he looking at? he’ll ask his wife, Marie.

The Parkinson’s syndrome diagnosis came decades ago, but it’s gotten worse in the last year or so. It snares his words as they come out of his mouth, shuffles his memories, sucks the power from his voice, and sends tremors through his gnarled hands.

Freddie, they love you, Marie reminds him.

When the ring announcer is introducing the fighters, he mentions Freddie by name—“The challenger, fighting out of the blue corner, trained by [louder now] Hall of Fame trainer Freddie Roach”—and the crowd—this is a boxing crowd, knows its stuff, knows what Freddie’s done for Manny over the years, knows about the Parkinson’s—roars its admiration.

Some of them are wondering if this is it for Freddie. Maybe that’s why they’re cheering so loudly for him. Parkinson’s is tough, and it’s mysterious, and maybe their dad or their aunt had it and they’ve seen what it can do. And Manny Pacquiao—what’s he doing, when you stop and think about it? Fighting a guy who could be his son, four years after his last fight, which was no beauty?

The two of them, Manny and Freddie, the old guy and the older guy, climb into the ring. You gotta wonder how much they have left in the tank.

But here’s the thing you have to remember, the thing any boxing crony will tell you, the thing Joyce Carol Oates and Norman Mailer and the rest of the writers knew: Boxers are illusionists. Their greatest skill is the fake-out. They want to fool you, make you think left and then—POP!—sneak in with a hard right. Play a trick. Find a hole. Find a way. Act tired, act beat, rope-a-dope…then bam-bam!, the one-two you never saw coming.

Freddie, yeah, has the slight droop in his face, and the shaking, and his foot tends to drag. And Manny, who once won a fight a few weeks before Barrios was born. But you know what? They look good, these two. Freddie moves quickly in the corner of the ring, rubbing his fighter’s shoulders, tending to him. Manny is bouncing, jabbing, his famous half-crazy eyes glaring at the opposing corner.

They look pretty good, these two. But boxers create illusions.

SIX-THRTY A.M. Freddie’s bedroom is windowless and dark, darker than dark. He lives in a new building in L.A., with valets and doormen and a gym and a pool. His bedroom is deep inside, at the end of the hall, a catacomb.

He squints his eyes open and lies there in the stillness. The rigidity sets in while he sleeps, and every morning if he doesn’t crack and bend his joints it feels like they might stop working for good. He stretches his legs under the sheets, moves his ankles. He broke his left ankle in the ’80s running with Virgil Hill, the first fighter he ever trained who became a world champion. The doctors performed tendon transfer surgery on the ankle, but it didn’t take—they botched it, Freddie is sure. It gives him a drop foot on that leg, and the ankle stiffens up.

He has dystonia in his neck that requires Botox injections every three months, and he rolls his head around on the pillow a little. He feels a tremor in his hands, which happens after the overnight meds have worn off. He extends his fingers the best he can, grips the sheets.

The stretches are important.

A man doesn’t feel the truth of age until he’s maybe 70. Before that you’re pretty much 19 years old for 50 years. It’s an evolutionary trick of the mind, and it helps us keep running and swimming and playing basketball and doing yoga and fighting for the welterweight title when those activities might otherwise seem foolhardy. We trust old rhythms. We repeat the rituals that have long shaped us. We see reminders every day that life is short but we don’t quite believe them. We believe we can keep going. And because we believe it, it feels true, it is true. Life is long, when it is long, because we convince ourselves it is long.

It’s Friday. The fight is a week from tomorrow. Today will be a long day. They’re all long. Freddie has a lot on his mind. One of his other fighters is coming in to train later in the morning, and he needs work. Another one of his fighters has a match in September and should be training hard now, but he’s got an eye injury. Marie has to talk to Freddie about some bank stuff later, and they’re flying to Vegas on Monday night, so he has to pack.

And Manny, of course. All he really should be thinking about is Manny. Manny’ll be running this morning, all the way up to the Griffith Observatory and back down, which Freddie thinks is too much at his age, but Manny does it anyway. This afternoon at the gym will be a zoo. Manny brings a lot of people with him—friends, family, some people Freddie doesn’t even know who they are. Freddie’s still a little ticked off about not being allowed to catch mitts for Manny, plus there’s tension between Freddie and Justin Fortune, Manny’s strength and conditioning coach, who has an uneasy relationship with Freddie.

Eventually Freddie hauls himself out of bed, plants his feet on the floor and presses down. His knees wake up as he stands. He walks slowly at first, a little janky, past his room-size closet, where stacks of T-shirts sit folded in perfect squares and sneakers are lined up like in a store, everything in order so he doesn’t have to think. He makes his way down the hall, past the signed Larry Bird and Bobby Orr jerseys hanging on the wall—Freddie’s from Dedham, Massachusetts, outside Boston. So’s his buddy Mac, asleep in the next room. Steve McDonough, but everybody calls him Mac. Mac’s not a fighter. They grew up together, and now Mac comes out to L.A. from Dedham for a few weeks at a time to hang out with Freddie. Mac’s a good friend.

In the kitchen Freddie pours himself a cup of coffee. Minnie, his tiny dog, hops up when she hears Freddie shuffling. Freddie’s place doesn’t have much view of the sky, so he can’t see the weather, but he knows it’ll be hot again today, and he’d better get Minnie out for a walk.

A week out, Freddie’s not nervous about the fight, exactly. Just anxious. This is what he does: trains fighters to fight, and they’re either ready or they’re not ready. Manny went with an eight-week training camp. Maybe should have been 10. Maybe should have been 12. Still, Freddie thinks Manny’s ready, or will be.

The gym opens at 8. It’s open six days a week and Marie and Freddie are both there together all day, every day, Freddie training fighters and Marie running things. Freddie sometimes even goes in on a Sunday, the one day it’s closed, gets a Starbucks across the street, and then sits at Wild Card alone, watching TV.

A week out, Freddie’s NOT NERVOUS about the fight, exactly. JUST ANXIOUS. This is what he does: TRAINS FIGHTERS TO FIGHT, and they’re EITHER READY or THEY’RE NOT READY.

The upstairs gym—the original space Freddie first rented 30 years ago—is the main gym. Regulars can pay $75 a month to train, walk-ins 10 bucks a day. They can spar in one of two rings or punch bags, and after the doors open each morning, the room fills with the dull thunk…thunk-thunk of the big bags and the rolling bapituh-bapituh-bapituh of the small ones.

Freddie’s in the downstairs gym now, waiting—the old laundromat space, now a private gym for his top competitive fighters. Pictures of Virgil Hill, of Cotto, of Chavez Jr., of Freddie at 12 years old wearing boxing shorts his mother sewed. Sylvester Stallone, Mickey Rourke, Mark Wahlberg, Russell Crowe, Edie Falco, and Bob Dylan have all come here to see the great Freddie Roach at work. Near the door there’s a photo of Freddie and the Reverend Jesse Jackson, who once spent a week at Wild Card working on his Parkinson’s with Freddie. When Jackson arrived, Freddie says, the reverend couldn’t climb the steps up to the gym. By the end of the week, the two men were racing up the steps.

Manny’s supposed to be here at 2 but sometimes he comes early. Freddie waits. It’s hard to concentrate on much else when Manny’s coming. Freddie cares deeply about the other fighters he’s training, who are all at different stages of their careers. They have fights scattered over the next few months, and he works them accordingly, depending on how far out they are from their fight: push them hard at first, then build their strength, work on their reflexes, harden their cores, and spar spar spar. But today they have to fight for attention in Freddie’s head.

In the corner, high on a shelf, sits a heavy vest meant to be worn while catching mitts. Catching mitts is one of the trainer’s most important jobs. He wears leather mitts on his hands, moving targets that the fighter punches hard and fast, sometimes for a good hour during a training session. The fighter is working on quickness, coordination, and power. It can hurt the trainer’s hands, and he catches stray punches to the kidney. You take a leak after a hard session catching mitts and it’s red with blood.

Freddie catches mitts for all his other fighters, but he doesn’t wear the vest.

As 2 o’clock approaches, some of Manny’s people start to arrive. Black SUVs and pickup trucks glide into the parking lot. The gym tenses up. Mac’s outside in the lot like a sentry, watching and waiting. Marvin, Freddie’s right-hand man, walks quickly, calmly in and out.

Freddie? Freddie’s leaning against a table, wearing his usual Wild Card T-shirt and shorts, waiting for his fighter, arms folded, mouth drawn, looking like he has something to say, and saying nothing. Gotta cover all the bases, he thinks. Gotta cover the bases. This guy Barrios, he’s fought some bad fights, but he’s fought some good fights. He’s getting ready right now too. We have to be extra ready. Cover all the bases.

Every afternoon, Manny climbs out of an SUV and strides into the gym, and every afternoon the energy is electric when he does. He’s surrounded by his people, mostly Filipino, 10 or 12 appendages all wearing Team Pacquiao shirts. Manny walks from the hot white sunlight into the familiar yellow glow of the gym. He sees a face he doesn’t recognize, smiles, and says, “Hi.”

One of his appendages holds up a towel while Manny changes into his shorts in the corner, and within a few minutes he’s in the ring doing mitts with a guy named Buboy Fernandez, a big-bellied man who grew up with Manny in the Philippines. Buboy doesn’t know much about boxing, but that doesn’t stop him from constantly barking at Manny in their native language as they work out together. Freddie’s in the corner, watching. He caught mitts for Manny for almost his whole career. They used to move around the ring together like one person, and when Manny’s gloves connected with Freddie’s mitts a hundred times a minute, it was like a machine gun.

Buboy’s a good guy, but Freddie has no idea what the fuck he’s saying to Manny and worries he might be giving him bad instruction.

He cocks his head into a quick tilt, which could be to ease the pain of the dystonia, or to correct the double vision into a single image, or because he’s annoyed. Or all three.

Manny’s sparring now, with another of Freddie’s boxers, a tough local kid named Saul Bustos, chosen by Freddie because his style and physique resemble Barrios’s—same height, same long reach. SEVEN DAYS BEFORE THE FIGHT

THE GYM IN the building where Freddie lives is shiny, lined with machines gleaming like they were just unwrapped, and has geometric floor tiles that squeak. Freddie meets his physical therapist, Sean Hampton, here three times a week—two if he can worm his way out of it.

Hampton is a sports and orthopedic PT who specializes in complex cases. He knows Freddie’s Parkinson’s, he knows the drop foot from the tendon surgery, he knows the dystonia and the double vision, and he knows the mood swings and the frustration, and the satisfaction Freddie feels when he can execute. Freddie stands still as Hampton fastens a drop foot brace around his ankle; it has strings that loop through the eyelets in Freddie’s Asics to keep the foot from dropping too low and a tensioner on the front to control the strings.

Hampton uses blood flow restriction training with Freddie, a technique for building muscle while lifting less weight and expending less energy. Around Freddie’s python biceps, Hampton straps tourniquets that look like blood pressure cuffs. When blood flows to the muscles, the bands block it from flowing back out through the veins, tricking the muscles into working harder with less weight. The muscles swell, due to the increased blood, and are more acutely deprived of oxygen than in a typical workout—two conditions ideal for hypertrophy, or added bulk.

Freddie’s body is like a piece of iron that’s been heated and bent, hammered and banged on, twisted and hammered again, all his life. He's one of six siblings and always found himself in scraps as a kid. He went to farm school in Walpole, the Norfolk County Agricultural High School, where he remembers killing cattle with a hammer and a gun. He worked for his father’s tree business as a chain saw operator. He spent hours spraying pesticides long before anyone used masks or gloves with that stuff. Later, one of his brothers had a business working on underground fuel tanks at gas stations. Freddie would climb down into the huge fiberglass tanks to seal them, no ventilation, no mask, the burning heat and the thick, noxious fumes almost knocking him out.

His father used to beat up his mother. As children, we think what we see at home is normal. Once, when Freddie saw that his mother had a black eye from his father’s fist, he laughed and asked her how the other guy looked. He was 11 years old.

He didn’t know better.

That was over 50 years ago. Freddie knows a lot now.

In the physical therapy session, Hampton has Freddie raising his arms over his head while stretching an elastic weight band with his hands, opening up his chest. Freddie’s arms are burning. He doesn’t say much to Hampton, does what he’s told.

Freddie fought 53 fights before becoming a trainer. He was diagnosed with Parkinson’s syndrome at age 27. Parkinson’s is a specific neurodegenerative disease that can cause problems with movement, motor control, and cognition, and it usually worsens over time. It weakens the production of dopamine by neurons in the brain, dopamine being an important neurotransmitter that helps us move and speak.

Marie says that some neurologists who’ve treated Freddie have speculated that his history with chemicals and fumes could have contributed to his diagnosis. On the Internet, a lot of people have assumed it’s from boxing, an opinion Freddie doesn’t share, and, according to Marie, neither does his primary neurologist. (For what it’s worth, the Internet also wrote on Freddie’s own Wikipedia page that his given name is Frederick. It’s Fred.)

“Usually, patients who have had the diagnosis as long as Freddie has, and have been on the medications as long as he has, start to lose upper back strength; they lose lateral musculature,” says Hampton. “They have a hard time initiating movements, especially once they stop moving—they get to a door or a step and have trouble moving again. He’s not like that. His whole body is strong as an ox. His work ethic is an 11 out of 10. When I give him an exercise and he knows it’s something he needs to do, he’ll go until he reaches complete exhaustion. If I let him, he would go to a point where he would injure himself. He has a massive tolerance for pain.”

After his workout, Freddie stops back home to pick up Minnie, and he and Mac and a couple of friends head to a nearby restaurant. The crowd is tourist chic: couples and families wearing their vacation dress-up clothes. Freddie is in a Wild Card T-shirt, ball cap, and shorts, Minnie mincing along at his feet. The hostess, the bartender, and every waitress who walks by smiles at Freddie, waves, hugs him.

At the table, Freddie, unsmiling, asks the waitress what he likes.

She puts a hand on his shoulder. She leans over and points on the menu to the vegetable stone-pot rice. “You got this last time, honey, with some steak on top,” she says slowly.

Freddie looks at the menu, jerks his head back up to the waitress. Her hand is still on his shoulder.

His voice is soft.

EVERYBODY’S LEAVING FOR Vegas today, and Freddie’s in a mood.

There are too many people around, for one thing. Manny won’t be here for hours, and there are already more cars K-turning around the parking lot than normal as Manny’s crew dribbles in. Anyway, Freddie’s in a mood, and his countenance doesn’t change much even when Manny arrives for one last workout at Wild Card before driving to Vegas. Manny sits against the wall wrapping his hands, one of boxing’s daily rituals. Freddie wraps all his other fighters’ hands, and when Freddie wraps your hand with gauze and tape, by the time he’s finished it feels like a part of your body, which is to say it feels like nothing at all. In a drawer by the ring: tidy stacks of gauze, tape, scissors, all arranged at perfect right angles between neat wooden dividers—so he doesn’t have to think.

The regulations state that a gauze wrap must measure at least 27 feet but not more than 49 feet long and must be 2 inches wide. A pad over the knuckles must use no more than 16 feet of 4-inch gauze. Quarter-inch-wide athletic tape may be applied between the fingers. The tape around the gauze must be between 8 and 15 feet long and no wider than 2 ¼ inches and be made of “stretchy cotton material with a Velcro closure.”

Freddie knows all of this, or rather he doesn’t need to know it, the way an Italian grandmother doesn’t need to know how many minutes the pasta should cook. HOW DO PEOPLE find each other, crawling around on this planet? How did Manny find Freddie, and would he have been a world champion in eight different divisions if he hadn’t?
