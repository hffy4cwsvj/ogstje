# So, You’re Not Gay, But You Want to Explore Your Sexuality. Now What?

Understanding and exploring your sexuality is often depicted in fiction as a one-off event, typically occurring in puberty. From Justin in Queer as Folk to Kurt in Glee to Charlie in Heartstopper, the tension and tenacity it takes to delve into—and act on—what are often considered shameful desires is arguably a right of passage for many queer men.

But new research from dating app Feeld’s Straight To A Point report and insight from sexuality experts at LGBTQ+ dating app Taimi show that self-identified straight men are exploring their sexuality at all stages of life, in ways that aren’t just focused around casual hookups or one night stands. On Feeld, straight, cisgender men initiate a small but meaningful share—just over six percent—of the connections they make with other men. Within that slice, nearly half (48 percent) of those man-to-man connections are between straight, cis men connecting with other straight, cis men.

Granted, Feeld bills itself as “the dating app for the curious” and allows users to explore connections of all kinds, such as matching with couples for sexual intimacy in what the app calls “constellations.” With more than monogamy on offer, it’s no surprise that Feeld attracts a more sexually open and fluid clientele. But 70 percent of the men connecting with other straight, cisgender men on Feeld identify as single.

So, what does “straight” really mean to these men? Why would they self-identify this way given their same-sex desires in these digital spaces, or are they making these matches as an exercise in sexual voyeurism? Is the use of labels like “straight” and “gay” even relevant anymore? Fundamentally, is this evidence that men are starting to understand their own nuances with sexuality in a new way? Men's Health spoke with sex experts and real men who've explored their sexuality to find out:

“Today, the boundaries [around sexuality] are a bit softer, but it still can feel ‘unmanly’ for men to explore their sexuality beyond straight lines,” says Edward Reese, gender and sexuality expert at Taimi. “So If you're curious, don’t be scared—what’s truly ‘unmanly’ is conforming to a society that tries to keep us in boxes.”

As Feeld’s data shows, men are rejecting the outdated notion that they must either be “closeted” or “out and proud” in their sexual identity because it doesn’t reflect their reality. “I stressed over labels a lot…whether I was pansexual or gay or straight,” says Jay, who describes themself visually as someone who “comes across as straight.” “I was scared to explore as I didn’t want to gaslight myself into ‘being queer’ or disrespect those around me who were sure [of their sexuality].”

Eventually, Jay’s experience as a cisgender man dating a non-binary person prompted them to start investigating their own identity. “They asked me if I had ever considered I was queer, considering my openness to dating queer people,” Jay recalls. “I initially thought no… however, dwelling on it and experimenting with just talking to people of the same gender, I realized that just because my attraction to people of the same gender was different to what I was used to, it didn't mean it wasn't genuine.”

The very essence of how people experience attraction has shifted on its axis over the past decade, with dating apps at the forefront of that change. The safety and discretion inherit to this digital landscape have allowed men to explore their physical and emotional desire to be intimate with other men without judgement—not only from others, but also themselves.

How to Explore Your Sexuality

Jay, Marcelo, and Fox ultimately found self-affirmation through sexual exploration, but their experiences exemplify how nerve-wracking it can be to navigate an identity shift, and that it's completely normal to feel that way. It doesn’t mean you’re doing something wrong or shameful, it just means you’re doing something new as a human being. So, here’s how to explore your sexuality on your terms:

1. Go on a solo sexploration.

Understanding what you desire sexually should begin with internal exploration, says New York-based sex therapist and certified sexologist Angel Suarez. “Try to learn about your body and become aware of the sensations that you enjoy holistically. Play with yourself in ways that include all parts of your body,” they say. “Don’t just relate pleasure to what you think are your typical sexual organs.”

For example, try slowing down solo exploration and using sensation-based tools, like feathers or objects that are cold or warm. Then, ask yourself how they feel, and if you like it. This activity doesn’t even need to be inherently sexual—just finding out what you enjoy when it comes to touch and pleasure on your own body is crucial if you’re going to take the leap into a new sexual experience. If you want to try something more explicitly sexual, experiment with nipple stimulation or explore anal pleasure with touch or sex toys.

2. Communicate your wants and needs.

“Focusing first on yourself allows you to be able to communicate what turns you on with men you engage with for the first time,” says Suarez. “Don’t believe the myths—men can also communicate what they want sexually; they just have to know what they want and don’t want.” This can look like asking the person you’re with how a certain sexual experience feels, which can then encourage you to share what you enjoy about that or another experience as well. Getting comfortable expressing what you do and don’t enjoy in the bedroom is another way to deepen your sexual exploration and understanding of self.

3. Be open to a new understanding of emotional intimacy.

Communication is key to navigating the emotional intimacy of sex with another man, which is the biggest challenge these “straight” men face, says Luke Brunning, a lecturer in Applied and Interdisciplinary Ethics at the University of Leeds and co-director of The Center for Love, Sex and Relationships. “In a heteronormative society like ours, same sex [intimacy] can prompt shame and insecurity, but also great joy and satisfaction,” he explains. “These strong feelings can shape our emotional connections in unexpected ways, even to the point of radically upending our sense of what romantic intimacy can be like… so be prepared for unexpected feelings.”

If such feelings come up during a sexual encounter, allow yourself to feel them. You may also need to signal to your partner using verbal or non-verbal cues that you need to take a break or stop entirely in order to respect those unexpected feelings. It can be a good idea to agree on that cue, also known as a safe word, before the sexual experience begins.

As we all continue to investigate our own desires in a world that, at times, attempts to divide us along gender and sexuality lines, we can hope that a greater male exploration of self will result in a cohort of men who are more respectful, tolerant, and confident in themselves—regardless of the label to which they might subscribe.
