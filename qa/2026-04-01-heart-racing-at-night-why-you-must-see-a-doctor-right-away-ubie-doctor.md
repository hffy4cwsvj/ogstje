Why Heart Racing at Night Requires an Important Doctor Visit

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Nighttime heart racing (nocturnal tachycardia) can be triggered by stimulants like caffeine or nicotine, side effects from medications or supplements, hormonal changes, anxiety, sleep apnea, or underlying arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation. Because causes range from benign to serious, a medical evaluation is recommended to identify the root issue and guide treatment.

Sleep aids and heart palpitationsMany people turn to sleep aids—both prescription and over-the-counter—to help with insomnia. However, some of these medications can overstimulate your heart:

• Diphenhydramine (found in many antihistamine-based sleep aids) can increase heart rate in sensitive individuals.• Melatonin by itself is usually safe, but combination formulas (with valerian root or alcohol-based tinctures) may trigger palpitations.• Prescription sedative-hypnotics (zolpidem, eszopiclone) can rarely lead to abnormal heart rhythms or anxiety-driven racing heartbeats.

If you suspect your sleep aid is linked to nightly palpitations, discuss alternatives—like cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) or relaxation techniques—with your doctor.

Why You Shouldn't Ignore Nighttime PalpitationsMost palpitations are benign, but when they occur at night, they can be a red flag:

• Interrupted sleep leads to daytime fatigue and worsened anxiety.• Frequent palpitations may indicate an underlying arrhythmia that could increase stroke or heart failure risk over time.• Untreated hyperthyroidism or anemia can have systemic consequences.• Episodes accompanied by dizziness, chest pain, or shortness of breath could signal a serious cardiac or respiratory event.

Key Warning SignsSeek prompt medical attention if your nighttime heart racing is accompanied by any of the following:

• Chest discomfort, pressure, or tightness• Fainting or near-fainting spells• Severe shortness of breath• Sudden weakness, numbness, or slurred speech• Rapid heartbeat lasting more than a few minutes

These symptoms could point to supraventricular tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, or even a heart attack. If you experience them, call emergency services immediately.

Self-Care and Lifestyle AdjustmentsAlongside medical evaluation, you can take steps at home to reduce nighttime palpitations:

• Limit stimulants: avoid caffeine, nicotine, and energy drinks after mid-afternoon.• Moderate alcohol intake: excessive drinking can trigger arrhythmias.• Stay hydrated: dehydration can cause electrolyte shifts that affect heart rhythm.• Practice stress reduction: deep breathing, meditation, or gentle yoga before bed.• Establish a sleep routine: go to bed and wake up at consistent times, create a cool, dark, quiet environment.

Debunking Common Myths• Myth: Only older adults get dangerous arrhythmias. Fact: While age is a factor, younger people—especially those with thyroid issues, caffeine overuse, or genetic predisposition—can also develop serious rhythm problems.

• Myth: If my heart flutters for a few seconds, it's harmless. Fact: Brief palpitations can be benign, but recurrent episodes should be evaluated to rule out treatable causes.

• Myth: Cutting out exercise will prevent palpitations. Fact: Regular, moderate exercise generally stabilizes your heart rhythm. Avoid sudden, extreme workouts if you're prone to arrhythmias.

When to Contact Emergency ServicesIf palpitations occur alongside any of these, call 911 or your local emergency number:

• Loss of consciousness or seizure• Chest pain radiating to jaw, arm, or back• Severe shortness of breath at rest• Sudden confusion, vision changes, or difficulty speaking

These signs could indicate a heart attack, stroke, or life-threatening arrhythmia.

Long-Term Management and Follow-UpAfter your initial evaluation, your doctor may recommend:

• Medication adjustments: beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, or antiarrhythmics to control rate and rhythm.• Ablation therapy: for certain supraventricular tachycardias resistant to medication.• Implantable devices: pacemakers or defibrillators in high-risk cases.• Ongoing monitoring: periodic Holter recordings or in-office EKGs.

Regular follow-up ensures that your treatment remains effective and safe.
