Explanation

Sleep problems are common, but when lack of sleep becomes persistent insomnia, your doctor may look beyond your bedtime routine. One of the first tests they often order is a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level. Here's why.

What Is TSH and Why It Matters

TSH is a hormone made by the pituitary gland in your brain.

It tells your thyroid gland (in your neck) how much thyroid hormone (T4 and T3) to produce.

When TSH is high, it usually means your thyroid is underactive (hypothyroidism).

When TSH is low, it often means your thyroid is overactive (hyperthyroidism).

Thyroid hormones regulate your metabolism, heart rate, body temperature and—importantly—your sleep-wake cycle.

How Thyroid Imbalance Affects Sleep

Hyperthyroidism and Insomnia

Increased metabolism can make you feel wired or anxious.

You may have trouble falling asleep or wake up frequently.

Night sweats and a racing heart can disrupt deep sleep.

Hypothyroidism and Sleep Quality

You may feel tired but still wake up exhausted.

You might experience muscle aches or cramps at night.

Some people with low thyroid function report insomnia, though they more often feel sleepy.

Because both overactive and underactive thyroid function can impact sleep in different ways, measuring TSH helps your doctor figure out if your thyroid is to blame.

Why Doctors Order a TSH Test for Insomnia

Screen for Underlying Causes

Many cases of insomnia are linked to stress, caffeine or poor sleep hygiene.

But if those factors don't explain your sleep loss, doctors check TSH to rule out thyroid disease.

Guide Further Testing

An abnormal TSH result can prompt measurement of free T4 and free T3 levels.

Thyroid antibody tests may follow if an autoimmune cause (like Graves' disease or Hashimoto's thyroiditis) is suspected.

Monitor Thyroid Treatment

If you already take thyroid medication, TSH helps ensure your dose is correct.

Over- or under-treatment can lead to sleep disturbances.

Thyroid Medication Causing Insomnia

Although thyroid hormone replacement helps normalize metabolism and energy, it can sometimes cause insomnia if not dosed properly.

Common Reasons Medication May Disrupt Sleep

Over-replacement: Taking too much levothyroxine or liothyronine pushes your thyroid levels into the "overactive" range.

Timing: Thyroid meds are best taken on an empty stomach first thing in the morning. Taking them later in the day can make you feel jittery at bedtime.

Drug Interactions: Certain supplements or medications (e.g., iron, calcium, proton-pump inhibitors) can affect thyroid hormone absorption, leading to fluctuating levels.

Individual Sensitivity: Some people are more sensitive to small changes in thyroid hormone levels and can develop symptoms of mild hyperthyroidism—including insomnia—even at doses considered "normal."

Signs Your Thyroid Medication Might Be to Blame

Heart palpitations or a racing pulse at night

Increased anxiety or restlessness

Weight loss despite normal eating habits

Excessive sweating or heat intolerance

If you notice these symptoms, discuss with your doctor whether your thyroid medication causing insomnia is a possibility.

What to Expect at Your Doctor's Visit

Detailed History

Your doctor will ask about your sleep habits, stress levels, diet, caffeine and alcohol use, and any medications or supplements you take.

Physical Exam

They may look for signs of thyroid enlargement (goiter), tremors, rapid heartbeat, or dry skin—clues to thyroid dysfunction.

Blood Tests

TSH: The first-line test.

Free T4 and Free T3: If TSH is abnormal.

Thyroid Antibodies: If an autoimmune thyroid disease is suspected.

Follow-Up

Based on results, your doctor may adjust your medication, recommend lifestyle changes, or refer you to an endocrinologist.

Lifestyle and Sleep Hygiene Tips

Whether your TSH is normal or treatment is underway, these strategies can help improve sleep:

Stick to a consistent sleep schedule—even on weekends.

Create a calming bedtime routine: dim lights, read a book, practice gentle stretches.

Keep your bedroom cool, dark and quiet.

Limit caffeine and nicotine, especially after midday.

Avoid screens (TV, phone, tablet) at least 30 minutes before bedtime.

Get regular daytime exercise—but not too close to bedtime.

Working with Your Doctor

Always tell your doctor about new or worsening sleep problems.

Do not adjust your thyroid medication dose without medical guidance.

Take-Home Messages

Persistent insomnia can have many causes, including thyroid dysfunction.

TSH is the first test your doctor orders because it offers a quick snapshot of thyroid health.

Both overactive and underactive thyroid function can disrupt sleep—though for different reasons.

Thyroid medication causing insomnia is possible if your dose is too high, timing is off or you have interactions.

Good sleep habits and careful monitoring of thyroid levels go hand in hand.
