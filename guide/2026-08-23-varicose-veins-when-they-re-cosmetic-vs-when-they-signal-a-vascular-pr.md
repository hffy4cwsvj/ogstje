Explanation

Varicose veins affect up to 30% of adults. They're enlarged, twisted veins—most often in the legs—that range from light blue to dark purple. For many people, varicose veins are a purely cosmetic concern: unsightly lines under the skin that don't cause pain or health risks. But in some cases, they're a warning sign of an underlying vascular problem, like chronic venous insufficiency (CVI), that can progress if untreated.

Understanding the difference between harmless varicose veins and those that require medical attention empowers you to take the right steps—whether that's lifestyle tweaks, a quick cosmetic treatment or a deeper evaluation by a vascular specialist.

What Causes Varicose Veins?

Healthy veins have one-way valves that keep blood flowing toward the heart. When these valves weaken or fail:

Blood pools in the vein

Pressure increases inside the vessel

The vein walls stretch, twist and become visible under the skin

Factors that raise your risk include:

Family history of varicose veins

Age (valves weaken over time)

Female gender and hormone changes (pregnancy, menopause)

Prolonged standing or sitting

Obesity

Previous leg injury or blood clots

Cosmetic Varicose Veins: Purely an Appearance Issue

Many people notice varicose veins when they first appear as lumpy, blue cords on the legs. If they're truly cosmetic:

Veins are mostly small (spider veins or mild varicosities)

No persistent pain, heaviness or cramping

No skin changes (no redness, rashes, ulcers)

Normal leg swelling (if any) resolves by elevating the legs

What you can do:

Wear opaque tights or concealing makeup

Use over-the-counter vein creams with horsetail or horse chestnut extract

Try self-care measures (see "Prevention & Self-Care" below)

Consider sclerotherapy or laser treatments with a cosmetic dermatologist

If appearance alone bothers you, minimally invasive treatments can fade or eliminate surface varicose veins with low downtime and high patient satisfaction.

When Varicose Veins Signal a Vascular Problem

Varicose veins go beyond cosmetic concerns when they reflect impaired blood flow. Signs to watch for:

• Leg heaviness or aching, especially after standing or at day's end• Burning, throbbing or muscle cramps in the calf• Chronic swelling (edema) that doesn't fully resolve overnight• Itchy or irritated skin over the vein

Evaluating Your Symptoms

If you suspect more than a cosmetic issue, a thorough evaluation is key:

Detailed medical history (family history, symptom timeline)

Physical exam while standing (to assess swelling, vein appearance)

Duplex ultrasound to map blood flow and valve function

Treatment Options

Conservative Measures

Leg elevation above heart level for 15–20 minutes, 2–3 times daily

Graduated compression stockings (15–30 mmHg or stronger if prescribed)

Regular low-impact exercise (walking, swimming, cycling)

Weight management and healthy diet

Minimally Invasive Procedures

Sclerotherapy: injecting a solution to close small veins

Endovenous thermal ablation (radiofrequency or laser) for larger varicosities

Ambulatory phlebectomy: removing surface veins through tiny incisions

Prevention & Self-Care Tips

While you can't change your genetics or age, you can adopt habits that reduce varicose vein progression:

• Change positions regularly—avoid standing or sitting for more than 30–60 minutes• Flex and point your toes periodically when seated• Sleep with legs slightly elevated or use a wedge pillow• Wear support stockings during flights or long drives• Maintain a healthy BMI and avoid tight clothing around the waist or thighs• Include fiber-rich foods to prevent constipation (which raises abdominal pressure)

These simple steps improve circulation and minimize the pressure on vein walls.

When to Talk to a Doctor

Seek professional care if you experience:

Rapidly worsening leg pain or swelling

Warmth, redness or tenderness along a vein (possible thrombophlebitis)

Open wounds or ulcers that don't heal in 2–4 weeks
