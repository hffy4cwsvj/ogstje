Lubrication vs. Repair: Choosing the Right Knee Injection

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Hyaluronic acid (HA) vs. platelet-rich plasma (PRP) knee injections: which is better? The right choice depends on speed of relief, duration, and cost.

HA injections: Restore joint lubrication, provide relief in 2–4 weeks, last 3–6 months, moderate cost, often covered by insurance.

PRP injections: Use your own platelets to promote tissue repair, take 4–6 weeks to work, last 6–12+ months, higher out-of-pocket cost.

Key factors to weigh include candidate suitability, safety profile, strength of clinical evidence, and insurance coverage.

Because knee pain can stem from many underlying causes—osteoarthritis, meniscus injury, tendonitis, or inflammatory conditions—the best injection choice depends on an accurate picture of what's driving your symptoms. Explanation

Knee pain from osteoarthritis or injury can make everyday tasks a struggle. When conservative treatments like physical therapy, weight management, and pain relievers aren't enough, injections can offer targeted relief. Two of the most popular options are hyaluronic acid (HA) injections—often called "lubrication"—and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections—aiming for "repair." Below, we break down what each involves, how they compare, and how to decide which might work best for you.

Understanding Knee Pain and Why Injections Matter

Your knee joint is cushioned by cartilage and bathed in synovial fluid, which acts like a shock absorber.

Osteoarthritis and some injuries thin cartilage and deplete fluid, causing pain, stiffness, and swelling.

Injections can reduce pain, improve mobility, and delay or avoid surgery.

What Is Hyaluronic Acid (HA) Injection?

Hyaluronic acid is a naturally occurring substance in joint fluid. HA injections (also called viscosupplementation) aim to:

Restore lubrication and cushioning

Reduce friction and shock on damaged cartilage

Improve range of motion

Typical regimen:

Series of 1–5 injections over a few weeks

Effects may appear in 2–4 weeks and last 3–6 months

Key points:

Widely studied and FDA-approved for knee osteoarthritis

Best for mild to moderate arthritis, especially if X-rays show joint space narrowing but not bone-on-bone contact

Relatively low cost and commonly covered by insurance

What Is Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) Injection?

PRP uses your own blood's platelets and growth factors to stimulate healing:

A blood draw is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets

The platelet-rich layer is injected into the knee

Growth factors may reduce inflammation and encourage tissue repair

1–3 injections spaced 1 month apart

Relief often begins in 4–6 weeks, lasting 6–12 months or more

Considered "off-label" by the FDA; guidelines vary by region

Emerging research supports benefits for mild to moderate osteoarthritis and some tendon/cartilage injuries

Costs are higher and insurance coverage is inconsistent

Who Is a Good Candidate?

HA injections may be right if you:

Have osteoarthritis confirmed by X-ray, but not severe bone-on-bone contact

Want a lower-cost, well-studied option

Prefer treatments covered by insurance

PRP injections may be right if you:

Are younger, active, or have sports-related cartilage/tendon issues

Seek a potentially longer-lasting effect and are fine paying out-of-pocket

Are willing to try a regenerative approach with evolving evidence

Always consider your overall health:

Blood disorders, infection risk, or platelet dysfunction may affect PRP suitability.

Allergy or sensitivity to components in HA products should be discussed with your provider.

Side Effects and Risks

Both treatments share common, generally mild risks:

Injection-site pain or swelling (24–72 hours)

Low risk of infection or bleeding

Rare allergic reaction (more with HA, depending on the product's source)

PRP–specific considerations:

Additional discomfort from blood draw

Variable platelet concentration can affect outcomes

Making Your Decision

Consult your orthopedic specialist or sports medicine doctor to review imaging and discuss goals.

Ask about your expected timeline: Do you need faster relief (HA) or longer durability (PRP)?

Verify insurance coverage and out-of-pocket costs.

Consider lifestyle factors: activity level, schedule for injection visits, recovery time, and follow-up care (e.g., physical therapy).

While injections are generally safe, any of the following warrant prompt medical evaluation:

Severe joint swelling with fever or chills

Conclusion

Choosing between lubrication (HA) and repair (PRP) injections for knee pain depends on your arthritis severity, pain relief timeline, budget, and willingness to try newer therapies. Both options can offer meaningful improvement in pain and function. Discuss your specific situation with an orthopedic specialist or sports medicine physician to make the most informed choice—and remember to seek immediate care if you experience severe or unusual symptoms.
